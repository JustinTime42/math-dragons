# Policy and Compliance for a Faceless, Partly AI-Produced Shorts/Reels Channel Promoting Math Dragons

Research date: 2026-10-19. Scope: YouTube Shorts + Facebook Reels, US-focused with brief EU/UK notes. Sources are labeled **[Official]** (platform or regulator text), **[Press]** (reputable news/trade press), or **[Commentary]** (blogs, SEO guides, law-firm notes). Many third-party "2026 policy" guides found in search were low-quality SEO content with unverifiable claims; they are cited only where flagged.

## 1. YouTube "inauthentic content" (ex-"repetitious content") and YPP rules on mass-produced / templated / AI content

### Takeaway
On July 15, 2025 YouTube renamed "repetitious content" to "inauthentic content" in its YouTube Partner Program (YPP) monetization policies, calling it a "minor update" that clarified mass-produced/repetitive content was already ineligible. It is a **monetization** policy applied at the **channel level**, not a Community Guidelines removal policy. AI use is not itself penalized. Templated, interchangeable output with no original insight is. Reach effects are indirect: the official text speaks only to monetization, though spam/deceptive-practices rules (separate) can cause removal.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] YPP page notice dated July 15, 2025: YouTube made a "minor update" clarifying the policy covers repetitive or mass-produced content, renamed from "repetitious content" to "inauthentic content," and stated this content was already ineligible for monetization. The section heading on the page still reads "Generic or Repetitive Content." — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] Definition: "Generic or repetitive content includes content that looks like it's made with a template." Not allowed to monetize: similar or repetitive content with low educational value or minimal variation; videos repeating the same situation with the same outcome; "Image slideshows, templated storylines, or scrolling text with minimal or no narrative"; content where videos feel interchangeable. — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] Allowed: videos sharing the same intro/outro as long as main content differs; series with similar formats where each video has a distinct storyline, focus, or concept. — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] Channel-wide: "channels where content feels interchangeable from video to video are not allowed to monetize." — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] AI: the policy names AI-generated content that uses generic or unoriginal templates to create an impression of mass production without the creator's original insight as not allowed; the page also says AI tools can be used if the final product reflects the creator's creative vision. — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Official] Separate "Reused content" policy (unchanged July 2025): channels that repurpose existing content without significant original commentary or substantive changes are ineligible; "applies even if you have permission from the original creator"; "applies to your channel as a whole"; "Monetization may be removed from your entire channel." Not allowed includes content that only reads aloud others' materials and "Promotion of other people's content, even with permission." Content that "mainly features the uploading creator" is allowed. — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- [Commentary] Third-party guides describe an "enforcement wave" against AI channels in early 2026, a January 2026 mass termination, and a Neal Mohan Jan 21, 2026 letter naming "AI slop"; one claims a July 16, 2026 clarification split the policy into three categories. None of these were verifiable from primary sources in this session. — [Flocker](https://flocker.tv/posts/youtube-inauthentic-content-ai-enforcement/); [UpTube](https://www.uptube.io/blog/youtube-new-policies-2026); [Clippie](https://clippie.ai/blog/ai-videos-banned-youtube-2026-rules); [lenspov](https://lenspov.com/articles/youtube-ai-content-demonetization-2026)
- [Commentary] Commonly flagged pattern per guides: AI narration over stock footage or AI imagery with no meaningful human oversight, posted at high volume with little variation. — [faceless.my](https://faceless.my/youtube/youtube-inauthentic-content-policy-faceless-ai-2026/); [ytzolo](https://ytzolo.com/blog/youtube-repetitive-content-policy-ai-slop-2026/)

### Inferences
- Math Dragons' own gameplay footage is first-party content that "mainly features the uploading creator," which is the safest category under reused-content rules. The main risk is the *inauthentic* bucket: a fixed template (same hook, same AI voice, same trivia structure, swapped numbers) produced at volume is exactly what "interchangeable" describes.
- Mitigations: vary formats (tip, myth-bust, gameplay walkthrough, parent Q&A, behind-the-scenes dev log), give each video a distinct concept/argument, include real human perspective (the developer's reasoning, design decisions, observed play-testing outcomes), avoid pure scrolling-text or slideshow formats.
- Since this channel's goal is app promotion, not AdSense revenue, losing YPP eligibility is a lower-stakes outcome than for a monetization channel. The bigger concern is whether templated content also gets weak recommendations; YouTube's official text does not say this, so treat it as plausible but unconfirmed.

### Gaps
- No official YouTube source found stating that inauthentic-content findings reduce Shorts distribution (vs. only monetization).
- 2026 updates (mass terminations, a July 2026 clarification) could not be confirmed from YouTube Blog / Creator Insider in this session.

## 2. YouTube altered/synthetic content disclosure (AI voice, AI animation, auto-labeling)

### Takeaway
Disclosure is required only for **realistic** altered/synthetic content (real people saying things they didn't, altered real events/places, realistic scenes that didn't happen, AI music as the main focus). Clearly unrealistic/animated content, such as fictional cartoon dragons, is exempt. YouTube may auto-label content made with its own GenAI tools, content carrying C2PA metadata, or content its systems detect, and those labels can't be removed. Disclosure itself does not limit audience or monetization.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Disclosure required for content that "seems realistic," including making a real person appear to say/do something they didn't, altering footage of a real event or place, generating a realistic scene that didn't occur, and creating music that's the main focus of the video. Examples requiring it include AI-generated music. — [YouTube Help: Disclosing altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Not required: unrealistic content (a unicorn ride in a fantastical world; an AI-generated missile in a fully animated video), minor edits, captions, idea generation, AI-generated scripts, titles, or thumbnails. "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs" is listed as not requiring disclosure. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Label placement: in the video player for AI-generated music or photorealistic content; in the expanded description for non-music AI audio or non-photorealistic/animated AI content. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Auto-labeling: YouTube may apply a label for content made with "YouTube's GenAI tools," content containing C2PA metadata, or content its systems detect. Labels from YouTube's AI tools, C2PA metadata, or manual review can't be adjusted. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Penalties: creators who consistently don't disclose "may be subject to manual application of a label, or penalties from YouTube," including content removal or YPP suspension. Disclosure alone doesn't limit a video's audience or affect monetization eligibility. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Press] YouTube's March 2024 launch announcement exempted "clearly unrealistic content, such as animation or someone riding a unicorn through a fantastical world." — [Music Ally, 2024-03-19](https://musically.com/2024/03/19/youtube-reveals-tool-to-label-altered-or-synthetic-content/)
- [Commentary] Guides state Veo / Dream Screen content in Shorts gets a permanent label. Plausible and consistent with the official "YouTube's GenAI tools" language, but the help page doesn't name Veo. — [air.io](https://air.io/en/youtube-hacks/youtube-ai-disclosure-in-2026-label-it-yourself-or-youtube-will); [aicleanify](https://aicleanify.com/blog/youtube-ai-content-labels-2026-c2pa-synthid)

### Inferences
- Cartoon dragons, stylized animation, and gameplay footage: no disclosure required.
- AI voiceover (a synthetic narrator, not imitating a real person) narrating math tips: the help page doesn't squarely address generic synthetic narrators (it only exempts cloning *your own* voice). Since a generic narrator doesn't depict a real person or a realistic event, it most likely falls outside the trigger. Ticking "yes" is low-cost (description-only label for non-photorealistic content, no reach/monetization effect per YouTube), so voluntary disclosure is a reasonable conservative default. **Ambiguous.**
- AI-generated background music as "main focus" requires disclosure; AI music as quiet background is arguably not the main focus. Ambiguous.
- Never generate realistic AI kids, realistic AI "parents giving testimonials," or realistic classroom scenes; those trigger disclosure and also raise FTC endorsement problems (Section 7).
- Assets from tools that embed C2PA (OpenAI image generation, Adobe Firefly, Google tools) may get auto-labeled whether or not you disclose.

### Gaps
- No official YouTube statement confirming SynthID (vs. C2PA) as a detection input.
- Whether Shorts display the label differently from long-form was not documented on the help page.

## 3. Meta: AI labeling, July 2025 originality crackdown, Reels distribution and demotion

### Takeaway
Meta may **require** an AI label on organic Facebook posts containing photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered; it auto-labels content carrying C2PA/IPTC signals; cartoon-style content doesn't need a label. On July 14, 2025, Meta announced that accounts that repeatedly repost others' content lose monetization temporarily and get reduced distribution across all posts. Original first-party content is unaffected. Engagement bait and clickbait remain on Meta's official demotion list.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Meta may require an AI label for content with photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created, modified, or altered, including with AI; this does not apply to images. Content without a required label may face penalties. Help page examples: a realistic AI-narrated reel needs a label; a cartoon-style landscape video does not. — [Meta Help: How to identify AI content on Meta products](https://www.meta.com/en-gb/help/artificial-intelligence/1783222608822690/) (fetched via search summary; exact current wording not directly verified)
- [Press] 2024 origin: Meta announced (Nick Clegg, Feb 2024) industry-standard-signal detection (C2PA/IPTC) and a self-disclosure requirement with possible penalties for non-disclosure. — [Axios, 2024-02-06](https://www.axios.com/2024/02/06/meta-ai-labeling-fix-oversight-board)
- [Official] Ads: Meta labels ads created or significantly edited with its own generative AI tools as "AI info." — [Meta Newsroom, Feb 2025](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/02/gen-ai-transparency-metas-ads-products/)
- [Press] July 14, 2025: accounts that repeatedly reuse others' videos, photos, or text lose access to Facebook monetization for a period and see reduced distribution on everything they post; duplicate videos get reduced distribution so the original creator gets the views; Meta is testing links on duplicates pointing to originals. Reaction videos, trend participation, and transformative edits are not targeted. Meta said it removed ~10M impersonator profiles and acted on ~500K accounts for spammy behavior/fake engagement. — [TechCrunch via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/following-youtube-meta-announces-crackdown-203001395.html); [Tubefilter, 2025-07-15](https://www.tubefilter.com/2025/07/15/ai-slop-unoriginal-repetitive-content-monetization-facebook-meta/); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-cracking-down-unoriginal-content-ai-replicas/753001/); [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/meta-follows-youtube-in-crackdown-on-unoriginal-content/551096/)
- [Commentary] Meta reportedly clarified in March 2026 that narration that only describes someone else's clip doesn't count as original. Not verified against Meta primary source. — [NoFaceTube](https://nofacetube.com/rules/meta-unoriginal-content-crackdown/)
- [Official] Meta's "Types of content we demote" page (updated July 2, 2025) lists Clickbait Links and Engagement Bait (under "Responding to People's Direct Feedback"), fact-checked misinformation, content likely violating Community Standards, and borderline content. The page doesn't mention AI-generated or unoriginal content specifically. — [Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/features/approach-to-ranking/types-of-content-we-demote/)

### Inferences
- Gameplay plus original animation and first-party voiceover is "original" under Meta's framing. Don't cross-post other creators' clips or trending meme videos with only a voiceover.
- Cross-posting the *same* first-party video to both YouTube and Facebook is not "reposting others' content." Meta's duplicate detection targets copies of others' work, though watermarks from other platforms (e.g., a TikTok logo) are a long-standing negative signal. Upload clean masters rather than downloaded re-exports. (The watermark point is from common practice knowledge, not a source found this session.)
- A realistic-sounding AI narrator over a cartoon may fall under "realistic-sounding audio." Meta's own example of a "realistic AI-narrated reel" needing a label suggests that disclosing AI voice is the safer default on Facebook. **Ambiguous.** The label does not appear to carry a distribution penalty, but Meta hasn't stated that explicitly the way YouTube has.

### Gaps
- No primary Meta source found on whether "AI info"-labeled organic Reels get reduced distribution.
- Meta's Facebook Content Monetization unoriginal-content guidelines text was not fetched directly.

## 4. Engagement-bait rules (both platforms) and safe alternatives

### Takeaway
Facebook demotes posts that goad interactions ("Comment YES if...", "Share with 10 friends...", "Tag a friend who...", react/vote bait), with repeat offenders facing Page-level demotion. YouTube explicitly allows asking viewers to like, comment, or subscribe; it prohibits **rewarding** engagement, sub-for-sub schemes, and forced engagement via polls/posts.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Meta lists Engagement Bait as demoted content. — [Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/features/approach-to-ranking/types-of-content-we-demote/)
- [Press, describing Facebook's 2017 policy] Five categories: vote bait, react bait, share bait, tag bait, comment bait. Facebook's own examples: "Comment 'YES' if you love rock as much as I do"; "Share with 10 friends for a chance to win a new convertible!"; "Tag friends who look like her..."; "LIKE this if you're an Aries or LOVE this if you're a Leo." Posts that ask genuine questions without dictating the answer are fine. Repeat Pages face stricter, Page-level demotions. — [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/facebook-demoting-engagement-bait/228071/); [Inc.](https://www.inc.com/minda-zetlin/facebook-is-demoting-massive-numbers-of-posts-will-yours-be-among-them.html)
- [Official] YouTube: "It's okay to ask viewers to like, comment, or subscribe." Prohibited: "Repetitive or templated content aimed at artificially inflating engagement through bots, coercion, or offering rewards"; coordinated "sub-for-sub"; "Exploiting community features, such as Polls or Image Posts, to force engagement"; "maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails"; content created solely to drive users to external sites (especially harmful ones). — [YouTube Spam, deceptive practices & scams policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973?hl=en)

### Inferences
- Safe alternatives: open questions ("What's the trickiest times table at your house?"), a math puzzle with the answer revealed in a pinned comment or follow-up video, "Save this for homework night," and a plain "Join the free beta, link in bio/description."
- Avoid giveaways tied to comments/shares/tags (Facebook share bait; YouTube reward-for-engagement), and avoid "comment DRAGON and I'll DM you the link" auto-DM funnels on Facebook (comment bait).
- Avoid thumbnails/titles promising results the video doesn't deliver (YouTube malicious clickbait; Meta clickbait).

### Gaps
- Current verbatim Meta engagement-bait guideline text (Content Distribution Guidelines page) not fetched; examples above derive from 2017 press coverage.

## 5. COPPA and YouTube "Made for Kids" (MFK)

### Takeaway
Dragon animation plus kids' game footage hits several of YouTube's MFK factors (animated characters, games that appeal to children, early-education subject matter). No single factor decides it. Audience intent, language, metadata, and actual audience matter, and YouTube's ML can override a creator's setting "in cases of error or abuse" with only one appeal per video. MFK disables comments, the notification bell, personalized ads, cards/end screens, save-to-playlist, and more. That would cripple a parent-marketing channel. FTC enforcement (Google/YouTube 2019, $170M; Disney 2025, $10M) shows mislabeling child-directed content as not-MFK carries real legal liability, so the channel must *genuinely* be adult-directed, not just labeled that way.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] YouTube MFK factors: subject matter (e.g., educational content for preschoolers); whether children are the intended or actual audience; child actors/models; "characters, celebrities, or toys that appeal to children, including animated characters"; language intended for children to understand; activities that appeal to children such as "play-acting, simple songs or games, or early education"; songs/stories/poems for children; other audience info including empirical evidence; whether content is advertised to children. No single factor is determinative. Family-friendly is not automatically MFK. General-audience examples: "Animated content that appeals to everyone," "A gaming video with adult humor," "A family vlog for parents." Creators bear legal responsibility; "Do not rely on our systems to set your audience for you." — [YouTube Help: Determining if your content is "made for kids"](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9528076?hl=en)
- [Official] Features disabled on MFK videos: autoplay on home, cards or end screens, video watermarks, channel memberships, comments, donate button, live chat, merchandise, notification bell, personalized advertising, Miniplayer playback, Super Chat/Stickers, save to playlist / watch later. Channel-level MFK also removes Posts. YouTube uses machine learning and may override audience choice "in cases of error or abuse"; overridden videos show "Set to Made for Kids" and can be appealed only once. — [YouTube Help: Features restricted on MFK content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9527654?hl=en)
- [Official] FTC COPPA FAQ: child-directed factors include subject matter, visual content, animated characters, child-oriented activities; the FTC recognizes some animated characters are directed to general audiences. — [FTC: Complying with COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)
- [Press] 2019 Google/YouTube settlement ($170M, with NY AG): FTC treated child-directed channels as online services; order required a system for channel owners to flag child-directed content; FTC noted creators who fail to properly identify child-directed content could face civil penalties. Vote 3-2. — [Help Net Security, 2019-09-05](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2019/09/05/youtube-coppa-violation/); [Dorsey](https://www.dorsey.com/newsresources/blogs/tmca/2019/09/google-and-youtube-to-pay-170-million-for-coppa-vi)
- [Press] Disney (announced Sept 2025): $10M civil penalty to settle DOJ/FTC complaint alleging it set "Not Made for Kids" at the channel level rather than reviewing individual videos, exposing under-13s to personalized ads/data collection; order requires a video-review program unless YouTube deploys age assurance. Finalization reported ~January 2026. — [Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/?p=354788); [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/disney-fined-10-million-for-collecting-childrens-data-on-youtube/); [iHeart/KFI, 2025-09-03](https://kfiam640.iheart.com/content/2025-09-03-disney-settles-10m-ftc-case-over-child-privacy-violations)
- [Official] 2025 COPPA Rule amendments: finalized Jan 16, 2025; separate verifiable parental consent required for disclosing children's data to third parties for targeted ads; data retention limited to what's reasonably necessary; biometric and government IDs added to "personal information"; safe harbor transparency; the FTC declined to finalize push-notification limits and school-edtech changes. — [FTC press release, 2025-01-16](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data)
- [Official] Federal Register: published April 22, 2025; amended Rule effective June 23, 2025; compliance deadline April 22, 2026 for most provisions (excluding § 312.11(d)(1), (d)(4), and (g)). — [Federal Register 2025-05904](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)

### Inferences
- **Real MFK risk.** A Short that is mostly bright dragon gameplay with simple counting, no adult framing, and kid-pitched narration looks MFK under YouTube's own factors, and YouTube's classifier can lock it.
- **Legitimately staying "not made for kids"** (meaning actually being directed at adults, not just labeled):
  - Adult-addressed voiceover and on-screen text ("Parents: here's why your 7-year-old struggles with subtraction"), adult vocabulary and reasoning, and parenting/teaching pain points as the subject.
  - Frame gameplay as a demo for a decision-maker (screen-in-hand, UI callouts, "what your kid sees vs. what it's teaching").
  - Make sure titles, descriptions, tags, and hashtags target parents/teachers/homeschoolers (#homeschool, #parentingtips, #teachersofyoutube) and avoid kid-targeted tags (#kidsvideos, #forkids, #cartoonsforkids).
  - No sing-along songs, nursery-style content, or "count with me" segments that invite kids to participate.
  - Don't run ads targeting children or post in kid-directed spaces.
  - Review and set audience per video (the Disney failure mode was a blanket channel-level setting).
  - Keep the channel's "actual audience" adult by avoiding formats kids binge (pure gameplay loops).
- **COPPA exposure for the channel itself.** The channel owner's COPPA risk comes from YouTube's data collection on a child-directed channel. Accurate designation is the control. Math Dragons' no-data-collection design means the app has minimal COPPA "operator" exposure, but marketing still matters: the 2025 amendments reportedly add "marketing or promotional materials or plans, representations to consumers or third parties, reviews by users or third parties" as evidence the FTC considers when deciding whether a service is child-directed. **This list comes from memory of the Federal Register text and was not fetched this session; verify it.** Math Dragons is plainly child-directed, so it must stay COPPA-clean in practice. Verify that no SDKs (analytics, crash reporting, Firebase) collect persistent identifiers. TestFlight and Play testing collect tester data under Apple/Google terms, and testers are adults who opt in.
- If a video does get set to MFK, comments and notifications go away and the Short mostly reaches the kids' recommendation cluster, which is the wrong audience for a parent funnel.

### Gaps
- No official YouTube statement on how Shorts specifically behave when MFK (the restricted-features page doesn't mention Shorts).
- Exact 2025 amendment text on the "directed to children" factors not fetched.
- No FTC case found against a small developer's marketing channel. The precedents are platforms and large studios.

## 6. Facebook equivalents for content about kids' apps

### Takeaway
Facebook has no MFK-style designation for organic Reels. Facebook and Instagram require users to be 13+, and Meta's demotion and ad-policy frameworks are the relevant controls. Meta's ads (not organic) prohibit targeting under-18s by interest and restrict ad targeting for teens. Organic content about a kids' app aimed at parents raises no special Facebook classification.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] Meta's demotion page has no child-directed or "made for kids" category. — [Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/features/approach-to-ranking/types-of-content-we-demote/)

### Inferences
- The main Facebook risks are general ones: originality, engagement bait, AI-label requirements for realistic audio, and music licensing. Child-safety rules apply if children are shown (Section 9).
- If boosting posts later, review Meta's teen ad-targeting restrictions and Special Ad Category rules. Target adults (parents), which aligns with the strategy anyway.

## 7. Advertising and endorsement rules; app-store beta promotion

### Takeaway
Promoting your own app on your own channel needs no "paid partnership" disclosure, since the connection is obvious from the brand being the channel. Any third party who is paid, gifted, or otherwise connected (a teacher influencer, a friend, a family member) must disclose the connection, and YouTube's "includes paid promotion" box applies to *third-party* sponsorship. Under FTC Section 5, objective claims ("improves math scores," "proven," "educational," "boosts grades") need competent, reliable substantiation *before* you make them. Endorsers must be bona fide users. Fabricated or AI-generated testimonials are high risk. TestFlight public links cap at 10,000 testers. Google Play closed testing only admits testers on an email list or Google Group, so a truly "public" link requires an open-join Google Group or switching to open testing.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] FTC Endorsement Guides revised June 2023 (published in the Federal Register July 26, 2023): endorser must have been a bona fide user when endorsing; objective claims conveyed through endorsements need the same substantiation as advertiser claims; material connections between endorser and marketer must be disclosed. — [FTC 2023 Endorsement Guides FRN](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/p204500_endorsement_guides_in_2023.pdf); [FTC: Endorsements, Influencers, and Reviews](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/advertising-marketing/endorsements-influencers-reviews)
- [Official] The 2023 proposed Guides included an example about a misleading picture of a child in an ad for a learn-to-read program (final retention not confirmed). — [FTC 2023 Endorsement Guides FRN](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/p204500_endorsement_guides_in_2023.pdf)
- [Official] FTC v. Lumos Labs (Lumosity), N.D. Cal., filed Jan 5, 2016, listed among FTC endorsement-related matters. — [FTC year in review PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/es/system/files/documents/public_statements/958883/160611yearinreview.pdf) (Note: Lumosity was a brain-training "improves performance" claims case; the claim details were not in the fetched source.)
- [Official] TestFlight: per-public-link enrollment limit can be set from 1 to 10,000; a public link requires a build approved by Beta App Review. — [Apple Tech Talk: Get started with TestFlight](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/tech-talks/110343/)
- [Commentary] Testers who join via public link count against the 10,000 external tester cap; links can be disabled. — [Centercode](https://www.centercode.com/blog/dont-need-10000-beta-testers); [betadrop](https://betadrop.app/blog/what-is-testflight-public-link/)
- [Official] Google Play closed testing: testers added via email lists (up to 200 lists x 2,000 users, 50 lists per track) or Google Groups ("Only members of that group can join"); shareable opt-in link appears once the app status is Published; testers can't leave public reviews on test versions; set a feedback URL/email on the opt-in page. — [Play Console Help: Set up an open, closed, or internal test](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9845334?hl=en)
- [Commentary] Personal developer accounts created after Nov 13, 2023 must run a closed test with ≥12 opted-in testers for 14 continuous days before production access. — [ExtendsClass](https://extendsclass.com/blog/google-plays-closed-testing-requirement-what-developers-need-to-know-in-2026); [Choicely](https://www.choicely.com/blog/google-play-12-tester-rule)

### Inferences
- **Claims ladder** (safe to risky):
  - Safe: describe features ("dragon-themed addition and subtraction practice," "no ads, no data collection").
  - Moderate: "designed with [curriculum standard] in mind," but only if true and documented.
  - Risky without studies: "improves math scores," "proven to," "kids learn 2x faster," "teacher-approved" (if implying an endorsement program).
  - "Educational" as a category descriptor is common and low risk. Specific outcome claims need substantiation.
- "No data collection" is an objective privacy claim. It must be strictly true, including third-party SDKs and the platforms' own analytics, or it becomes a deception risk. "No ads" must also hold.
- Never use AI-generated "parent testimonials" or realistic AI parents/teachers presented as real users. That violates the bona-fide-user principle and YouTube's synthetic-content rules.
- Testers/reviews: don't offer rewards for positive reviews or ratings. Rewarding beta sign-ups (e.g., a giveaway) is murkier, and it also counts as YouTube reward-for-engagement or Facebook share bait if tied to comments/shares. Google Play and Apple both prohibit manipulated or incentivized ratings in their developer policies (from general knowledge, not fetched this session).
- Google Play "public join link": if the closed track uses a Google Group set to "anyone can join," the flow is: join group, then opt in, then install. That's a two-step friction point to explain in video CTAs. Open testing is the native public option and is listed on Play (verify the trade-offs). Check the current track setup.
- TestFlight: 10,000 is a hard ceiling for external testers. If a Short goes viral, the link fills and stops accepting. Set link limits deliberately and keep a waitlist/landing page as the CTA destination so you're not tied to a single link.
- Apple builds expire 90 days after upload (general knowledge, not sourced here), so evergreen Shorts linking straight to TestFlight may point to a broken experience later. A landing page fixes this too.

## 8. Music copyright in Shorts/Reels (business Pages vs. creators)

### Takeaway
On Facebook, Meta's popular-music licenses are for personal/non-commercial use. Business Pages (which a Math Dragons Page is) should use only the Meta **Sound Collection** or independently licensed or original audio. On YouTube, Shorts' built-in audio library is the cleared path. For a promotional channel, the cleanest choices are original or AI-generated music you own (note YouTube's disclosure rule for AI music as the "main focus") or royalty-free libraries with commercial licenses.

### Cited Findings
- [Commentary] Meta's licensing agreements for popular music generally don't cover commercial use; Business Pages face stricter rules; the Sound Collection is pre-cleared for commercial use on Facebook/Instagram including ads; unlicensed commercial use can lead to muting, removal, or Page restrictions. — [jeffbullas.com thread](https://www.jeffbullas.com/thread/what-are-the-rules-for-using-popular-music-in-facebook-stories/); [postlinkapp](https://postlinkapp.com/blog/how-to-add-music-to-a-facebook-post)
- [Commentary, law firm] Osborne Clarke (March 2026) notes repeated "waves of warning letters" over unlicensed music in business social media posts (EU/German context); appearing in a platform library doesn't by itself confirm commercial permission. — [Osborne Clarke](https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/use-music-social-media-platforms-what-businesses-and-creators-need-know-about-usage); [IT-Recht Kanzlei](https://www.it-recht-kanzlei.de/nutzung-musik-reels-instagram-urheberrecht.html)
- [Official] YouTube requires disclosure for AI-generated music that is the main focus of a video. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)

### Inferences
- Use the project's own generated music (the repo has a `/generate-music` pipeline). Keep licensing records for the generation provider's commercial terms.
- Avoid trending commercial tracks entirely on the Facebook Page. On YouTube, using trending audio from the Shorts library is allowed in Shorts, but those tracks can't be reused in long-form, and claims may route revenue elsewhere (general knowledge).

### Gaps

- YouTube Shorts audio-library licensing terms for brand channels were not fetched.

## 9. Showing children in videos; AI-generated children; child-safety policies

### Takeaway
Child actors are an explicit YouTube MFK factor, so showing real kids raises MFK risk and triggers child-safety considerations: YouTube restricts comments on many videos featuring minors, and parental consent and privacy are involved. Realistic AI-generated children trigger YouTube's synthetic disclosure (a realistic scene that didn't happen) and Meta's labeling, and they read as fabricated endorsement if presented as real users. The safest approach is no children on screen. Show only hands-on-device shots, or animated characters, and keep adult framing.

### Cited Findings
- [Official] "Whether the video includes child actors or models" is an MFK factor. — [YouTube Help: Determining MFK](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9528076?hl=en)
- [Official] Realistic generated scenes that didn't occur require disclosure. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- [Official] Meta may require an AI label for photorealistic AI video. — [Meta Help](https://www.meta.com/en-gb/help/artificial-intelligence/1783222608822690/)

### Inferences
- If real kids are ever filmed: get written parental consent, no names/schools/locations, no bath/swimwear or bedtime scenes, frame shots on the device, and expect comment restrictions on YouTube.
- Avoid realistic AI children entirely. Combined with a kids' app, they risk MFK classification, disclosure labels, endorsement-deception issues, and child-safety classifier scrutiny.

### Gaps
- YouTube's child-safety policy page (comment disabling on videos with minors) and Meta's child-safety and AI-generated-minor rules were not fetched this session. Verify before any child-on-camera content.

## 10. EU/UK differences (brief)

### Takeaway
The EU AI Act Article 50 transparency obligations (including disclosure of deepfakes by deployers) applied from August 2, 2026. The Digital Omnibus delayed high-risk obligations, not Article 50, though sources conflict on a grace period for machine-readable marking. For a US channel using clearly fictional animation, the practical impact is small: platform labels plus not making realistic deepfakes covers it. The UK Children's Code (Age Appropriate Design Code) governs the *app's* design if offered to UK children, not the marketing videos.

### Cited Findings
- [Press] Article 50 transparency obligations took effect August 2, 2026, as scheduled; the Digital Omnibus delayed other parts of the Act. — [Al Jazeera, 2026-08-06](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/8/6/what-came-into-force-with-the-eus-ai-act-this-week-and-what-didnt)
- [Commentary] Digital Omnibus (Reg. (EU) 2026/1744) entered into force July 27, 2026, pushing Annex III high-risk obligations to Dec 2, 2027 and Annex I obligations to Aug 2, 2028; one source lists Article 50(2) machine-readable marking moving to Dec 2, 2026 for systems already on the market (conflicting/unverified). — [compliancehub.wiki](https://compliancehub.wiki/eu-ai-act-article-50-transparency-digital-omnibus-2026/); [lobsterpack](https://www.lobsterpack.com/blog/eu-ai-act-omnibus-delay-2027/)

### Inferences
- Article 50(2) marking falls mainly on AI *providers* (the model vendors). Article 50(4) deepfake disclosure falls on *deployers* and covers realistic content resembling real persons, objects, places, or events. Cartoon dragons are not deepfakes. Keep platform AI labels on for any realistic AI audio to stay aligned.
- UK: the Children's Code applies to the app if likely accessed by UK children. The no-data design helps. Marketing videos aimed at adults aren't the target of the code.

### Gaps
- Official EU text on Article 50 timing not fetched; UK Children's Code applicability to marketing not researched in depth.
