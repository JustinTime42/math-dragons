# Route video-scrolling parents into the beta

> **Correction (2026-10-19):** the "Firebase conflict" this report raises is resolved. The app has no Firebase code or packages (verified 2026-10-19); "the app collects no data" is supportable, scoped to the app (the website's form, waitlist and analytics are covered separately in the privacy policy). Justin decided 2026-10-19 that lifetime free access goes to everyone who joins the beta and installs; the spec must word it specifically and honor it (see PLAN.md beta perk).


As of October 19, 2026, playmathdragons.com should be a **static, parent-addressed, platform-aware Astro site deployed as a Cloudflare Worker with static assets**. Its home page has one job: get a parent who just tapped through from a Short or Reel through TestFlight (two steps) or Google Play closed testing (three steps) without losing them. Every other page (origin story, FAQ, privacy, support, feedback) exists to make that one job trustworthy and store-compliant. The conversion evidence favors short copy at a 5th-7th grade reading level, a single dominant CTA with objection-killing microcopy ("Free. No ads. No accounts.") right under the button, and a hero that visibly matches the video the parent just watched. The biggest UX risk is **Facebook's in-app browser**, which has no Google session and can break store hand-offs; YouTube opens links in the default browser, Facebook does not. The biggest Android failure is a parent joining the Google Group with one Google account and opening Play with another, so that warning is the most valuable line of microcopy on the page. On compliance, a site written to adults, using Tallyclaw as illustration rather than as an interactive guide, keeps COPPA attached to the app (which collects nothing) rather than the site. One privacy policy with separate "app" and "website" sections satisfies Apple, Google and CalOPPA. No store badges may appear until the app is publicly listed. Claims must stay descriptive ("practice", "adaptive"), never outcome-based ("improves scores"). The stack costs $0-9 a month: Cloudflare static hosting is free and unlimited, Umami Cloud's free tier or Plausible at $9 a month handles custom events, and a tiny Worker covers the form and outbound-click counting. Several load-bearing behaviors, especially TestFlight and Play links inside Facebook's webview, are undocumented and must be tested on real devices before the first promo video ships.

Source-type labels used below: **(official)** means platform or regulator text fetched on 2026-10-19; **(commentary)** means law-firm or press analysis; **(vendor)** means a commercially interested source; **(secondary)** means an aggregator or blog; **[not re-verified this session]** means recalled rather than fetched; **(inference)** means the research team's reasoning, not a sourced fact.

## Parents decide in one screen, so the hero must match the video and remove objections

### The benchmarks reward short, simple copy and expect YouTube to convert below Facebook

The best broad dataset available is Unbounce's 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report: **41,000+ landing pages, 57M+ conversions, 464M visitors, median conversion 6.6%**, which Unbounce frames as a baseline rather than a target ([Unbounce 2024](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)). Three of its findings map directly onto this site. Pages written at a **5th-7th grade reading level convert at 11.1%**, more than double professional-level copy (5.3%), and difficult words, reading time and word count all correlate negatively with conversion. **Mobile accounts for 83% of visits but desktop converts about 8% better**. Paid-social traffic converts at **17.9% (Instagram) and 13% (Facebook), while YouTube, TikTok and X fall between 6% and 9%** (all from [Unbounce 2024](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)). These are generic goals (form fills and clicks), not multi-step app installs, so a TestFlight or Play beta will convert far lower end-to-end. The practical consequence (inference) is to measure each step separately (landing visit, platform button tap, return to step 2, opt-in) rather than chase a single headline rate. It also means YouTube-sourced visitors will look worse than Facebook visitors even if the page is fine.

The reading-level finding lines up with the project's existing "talk to adults, not kids" rule. Simple is not childish: short sentences addressed to a busy parent ("Your kid practices math. You don't have to referee it.") satisfy the data and the compliance posture at the same time.

### Competitors split between product-led and worry-led heroes, and the worry-led one fits best

Four competitor pages fetched on 2026-10-19 show the range. **Khan Academy Kids** leads with "Joyful Learning Starts Here!", a video thumbnail of a child on a tablet, three store badges with nothing competing, and "No ads, no subscriptions" in the section immediately below the hero ([Khan Academy Kids](https://www.khanacademy.org/kids)). **DragonBox** speaks to the parent in second person ("for you and your child to share"), shows an age band per app, and puts no trust or privacy claims above the fold ([DragonBox](https://www.dragonbox.com/)). **Prodigy** splits CTAs by audience (teachers vs parents) and stacks trust heavily: a commissioned survey stat ("91% of parents said their children enjoy Prodigy Math"), role-labeled testimonials, Mom's Choice and Parent and Teacher Choice awards, and a Common Sense privacy seal ([Prodigy](https://www.prodigygame.com/main-en/)). **Synthesis Tutor** is outcome-led and parent-worry-led ("Your child's personal math tutor." / "Warm, patient, encouraging."), with a single "Try it for free" CTA and "No credit card necessary" placed directly under the button, an age band ("Designed for ages 5-11"), and its founder story far down the page ([Synthesis Tutor](https://www.synthesis.com/tutor)).

The Synthesis structure is the closest fit for Math Dragons (inference): an outcome headline aimed at the parent, one CTA, and microcopy attached to the button that kills the main objections. Math Dragons' genuine differentiator is "no ads, no accounts, the app collects nothing," so that promise belongs under the button rather than one section down as Khan places it. Synthesis also supplies a cautionary example: its page claims both 35,000 and 25,000 families, an inconsistency a skeptical parent can spot ([Synthesis Tutor](https://www.synthesis.com/tutor)). No controlled study was found comparing problem-led and product-led headlines, or hero video loops against screenshots, for mobile app pages; that is an A/B question for the campaign variants, not a settled answer.

### Message match is the cheapest lever a static site has

The principle that a landing page should repeat the ad's promise, wording and look is well established in CRO practice. CXL argues that missing "scent" explains underperformance more often than structure or copy, and that ad and page copy should be nearly identical ([CXL](https://cxl.com/blog/give-your-advertising-roi-a-serious-boost-by-maintaining-scent/)). The evidence is practitioner-grade and from the 2010s, and the widely repeated statistics ("73% mismatch", "66% lift") have no verifiable primary study ([Webtonic](https://www.webtonic.io/blog/message-match)). No study covers short-form video specifically. Even so, the cost on a static site is near zero (inference): each campaign path (for example `/yt1`) can render the same page with that video's headline, the same mini-game and dragon in the first hero frame, and a matching Tallyclaw line, while the CTA and install steps stay identical across every variant. The parent's first glance should confirm "this is the thing from the video" before they read a word.

### An unknown solo developer earns trust with checkable specifics, not seals

A new indie product has none of Prodigy's third-party proof, so its trust signals have to be things a parent can verify (inference). Three work. First, state privacy as specific facts ("No ads. No accounts. No chat. No purchases. The app collects no data.") and link to the App Store privacy label and Play Data safety section as corroboration. Second, put a one-line origin hook ("Built by a dad, for his own kids") at or just under the fold and the full story on its own page, since Synthesis shows founder stories work lower down. On a faceless site that can mean a signed first-person note, photos of hands, desk or notebook sketches, or Tallyclaw "introducing" the developer. Third, use social proof only when it is true and attributable: a first name plus "parent of a 7-year-old", and live counts such as "Founding beta: 37 families so far" rather than invented round numbers. No NN/g or Baymard research on trust signals for unknown developers or children's products was found within the research budget; those claims should not be cited until sourced.

## Two store flows, three Android steps and one account-mismatch trap

### Each platform's join flow has a distinct, documented failure mode

On iOS, external testers install Apple's TestFlight app and then open the public link. Public links allow up to **10,000 external testers per app**, can be capped per group, can be "Open to Anyone" or filtered by device and OS version, and show testers anonymously while still reporting installs, sessions and crashes; the first build needs TestFlight App Review, and anyone can reshare the link (official) ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/test-a-beta-version/invite-external-testers)). Builds expire **90 days after upload**, and the common public-link errors map to config problems: "This beta is full", "no longer available" (expired build), "Page not found" (disabled link), and "You're not invited" (wrong link or Apple ID, fixed by opening the exact link in Safari) (vendor, undated) ([TF Invites](https://www.tfinvites.com/blog/testflight-public-link-not-working)). Developers have also reported public links failing when the account had previously been invited by email (anecdotal) ([Apple Developer Forums 718229](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/718229)).

On Android, "for closed tests using a Google Group, users must join the group before opting into your test"; each tester opts in through the link, which appears only once the app is "Published" and "can take several hours to become available" after first publish. Testers see an explanation of their responsibilities and the developer's feedback channel on the opt-in page, closed-test apps are not searchable, and testers cannot leave public reviews (official) ([Google Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9845334)). A September 2026 developer write-up identifies the dominant failure: the Google account that joined the group differs from the account signed into Play, producing "App not available". Other failures are using the store listing URL instead of the opt-in URL (the correct one starts `play.google.com/apps/testing/`), the download link taking a few minutes to work, and the rule that an email list and a Google Group cannot both be active on one track (secondary) ([DEV Community, Sep 2026](https://dev.to/zenith605/google-play-closed-testing-joined-the-group-but-opt-in-says-app-not-available-6-checks-3dm3)). Vendor sources add that some default browsers do not pass the Google session correctly, and recommend opening the opt-in link in Chrome signed in with the same account (vendor) ([PrimeTestLab](https://primetestlab.com/blog/google-group-closed-testing-not-working)).

The Play production gate raises the stakes on Android. Personal developer accounts created after November 13, 2023 need **at least 12 testers opted in continuously for 14 days** (official) ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14151465)), and opting out and back in restarts a tester's count (secondary) ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/zenith605/google-play-closed-testing-joined-the-group-but-opt-in-says-app-not-available-6-checks-3dm3)). A parent who gets stuck and retries with a different account usually ends up lost as a tester.

### The page should be a stepper that survives leaving and coming back

Every step of both flows leaves the site, so the install section works best (inference) as a numbered stepper where each step is a self-contained button that still works when the parent returns, with no state that must persist between visits. Platform detection decides which path is shown first, never which path exists. The iOS path reads: (1) "Get TestFlight, Apple's free beta-testing app"; (2) "Come back here and tap Join the Math Dragons beta"; (3) "Tap Install in TestFlight". The Android path reads: (1) "Join the tester group" with the note "Use the same Google account your Play Store uses"; (2) "Tap Become a tester"; (3) "Install from Google Play". A small screenshot of each step's destination screen shows the parent what success looks like before they tap away. A collapsed "Did it say App not available?" troubleshooter covers account mismatch, "wait a few minutes", and "open this in Chrome". Configuring the Google Group to auto-approve makes Android step 1 instant. The Play Console feedback channel should point to the site's feedback page, because Google displays it on the opt-in screen.

Tallyclaw belongs in this section as a voice, not a guide (inference). A gruff veteran's dry aside to the parent ("Wrong Google account. Most common mistake in the realm. Check the little circle in the corner.") makes the troubleshooter memorable without inviting a child to interact with the dragon, which matters for the COPPA analysis below.

The page also carries operational duties the video funnel created. Evergreen videos will keep sending parents for months, so the TestFlight build must never lapse; the companion video report recommends shipping a new build every 60-75 days and recruiting 25-40 Android testers so drop-offs never pull the count below 12. If a build does lapse, the site needs a one-line status banner ("New iPhone build arriving this week; join the list and we'll tell you") rather than a dead link.

### YouTube hands off to a real browser and Facebook does not

A maintained open-source in-app-browser test matrix (iOS rows updated March 7, 2026; Android rows November 7, 2024) reports that **YouTube and Gmail open links in the default browser on both iOS and Android**, while **Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, TikTok and Threads use their own in-app browsers** ([shalanah/inapp-debugger](https://github.com/shalanah/inapp-debugger)). On iOS, Facebook supports the `x-safari-https://` escape scheme only via `window.open`; on Android, Facebook and Instagram honor an `intent://` escape to the default browser, and TikTok is "not dependable" (same source). The matrix also warns that tapping the Safari icon inside YouTube routes into SFSafariViewController rather than Safari proper. Vendor sources add that Universal Links hand off only on a user-initiated navigation inside these webviews, that Android WebViews may ignore `intent://`, and that the reliable workaround is an interstitial with an "Open in browser" button designed around a real tap (vendor) ([WarpLink](https://warplink.app/blog/deep-linking-in-app-browsers); [appy.to](https://appy.to/blog/in-app-browser-deep-links)). Host-app browsers are sandboxed and logged out (vendor) ([u2l.ai](https://u2l.ai/blog/youtube-link-opens-in-browser)), which is exactly what breaks the Android flow: no Google session means the group join and opt-in run under no account or the wrong one.

This matters more than it first appears, because the companion video report routes Facebook traffic through a link in the Page's first comment, and every one of those taps opens inside Facebook's webview. The site should detect Facebook and Instagram webviews by user agent (`FBAN`/`FBAV` plus `FB_IAB`/`FB4A` on Android and `FBIOS` on iOS; `Instagram <version>`) ([Bankrate production script](https://www.bankrate.com/v6.402.0/next/build/assets/isMetabrowser-Bhx5FPe_.js); [inapp-spy README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/inapp-spy@5.0.10/README.md)). It should then show a top banner: "You're inside Facebook's browser. Tap the menu, then Open in Chrome/Safari, so the install works." On Android the banner offers an `intent://` escape button, and the Android stepper sits behind it. On iOS, a tap-initiated `x-safari-https://` attempt goes first, with the manual instruction as fallback (inference). Whether TestFlight and App Store links hand off correctly from Facebook's iOS webview on a real tap is undocumented by Meta, Apple or Google, and the Android matrix rows are almost two years old. Both questions go on the real-device test list.

### Desktop visitors get one QR code, not two

A 2024 U.S. Census Bureau usability study found all 20 participants scanned a QR code successfully with an average time-on-task of **12.4 seconds**, with the caveat that none lacked prior QR experience ([Census Bureau RSM2024-05](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2024/adrm/rsm2024-05.html)). On desktop, the stepper should be replaced (inference) by a single QR code pointing to an attributable path such as `/qr`, which lands the phone on the platform-detected stepper. That avoids printing separate iOS and Android codes. The short typed URL sits next to it, with an optional "email me the link" field. No data was found comparing QR against send-to-phone email.

### Email is the fallback and the value exchange is stated, not implied

No authoritative source was found on email capture as a beta fallback or on double opt-in conversion effects. Email-sourced traffic does convert best of all channels in Unbounce's 2024 data, at a reported 19.3% median (secondary summary, not verified on the primary page) ([MarketingProfs](https://marketingprofs.com/charts/2024/52374/landing-page-conversion-benchmarks)), which supports using the list to bring stalled parents back. The waitlist serves three uses (inference): the desktop "send me the link" path, the fallback for parents on the wrong device or stuck mid-flow, and the channel for launch news and feedback asks. It must stay visually secondary to the install CTA.

The value exchange should sit next to the CTA in plain words, consistent with the commitment the video report recommends: "You get the full game free, early. We ask: your kid plays 3 times in 2 weeks, and you answer one 2-minute survey." The welcome survey should also carry the "where did you hear about us?" question that backs up path-based attribution. Recognition perks such as opt-in credits, a beta-only cosmetic, or a vote on the next dragon are common, but no sourced evidence of their effect was found, and their wording is constrained by the store rules below.

## A parent-directed site keeps COPPA attached to the app

### The 2025 COPPA amendments made marketing materials evidence of audience

COPPA defines a child-directed service as a commercial site "or portion thereof, that is targeted to children" and weighs "subject matter, visual content, use of animated characters or child-oriented activities and incentives, music or other audio content, age of models," language, and whether advertising is child-directed. The amended rule adds "evidence regarding the intended audience, including marketing or promotional materials or plans, representations to consumers or to third parties, reviews by users or third parties" (official text via the LII mirror) ([16 CFR 312.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.2)). The amendments were published April 22, 2025, effective June 23, 2025, with full compliance due **April 22, 2026** (commentary) ([Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/2025/05/ftc-publishes-updates-to-coppa-rule)), and the FTC said it does not expect operators to continuously monitor all such evidence (commentary) ([Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-coppa-rule-changes-in-the-biden)). The FTC FAQ adds that a site is not child-directed "just because some children visit", that "no single factor is determinative", and that COPPA "does not cover information collected from adults that may pertain to children" (official) ([FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)). Linking to a child-directed app does not by itself make a site child-directed (official) ([16 CFR 312.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/312.2)).

The likely regulatory picture (inference) is that the app is plainly child-directed but collects nothing, while the site is a separate question decided by the same factors. Parent-addressed copy, an origin story, an FAQ and a privacy policy point toward a general-audience site. The dragons push the other way, because "animated characters" is a named factor and the definition covers a child-directed "portion". A kids' corner, a playable demo, a coloring page, a "say hi to Tallyclaw" prompt or a quiz would create exactly that portion. Under the 2025 evidence language, "send your kid here" copy or promotion on kid-heavy placements also counts. The design rule follows: Tallyclaw appears as illustration and as a dry voice speaking to adults, the site has no child activities or incentives, testimonials come from parents, forms say "for parents and teachers", future SEO articles target parents and educators, and no site ads run on child-directed placements. This is the same discipline the video report requires to keep YouTube from classifying the channel as Made for Kids, so the brand rule can be shared.

If a child writes through the contact form anyway, the one-time-response exception applies: the operator may reply once and must then delete the child's contact information "promptly", or "immediately" if not responding, and such a message "may give you actual knowledge" of collecting a child's data (official) ([FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)). The form should say "This form is for parents and other adults. If you're under 13, please ask a parent to write to us", and a written procedure should cover deleting the message from the inbox and form backend. The waitlist is the reason the parent-directed posture matters most: on a child-directed site, a waitlist email from a child would need verifiable parental consent because it is not a one-time response (inference).

### One privacy policy with separate app and website sections satisfies every store and CalOPPA

Apple requires a privacy policy link in App Store Connect and in the app that identifies "what data, if any, the app/service collects", confirms equal protection by any third party, and explains retention, deletion and consent revocation. Guideline 1.5 requires that the app "and its Support URL include an easy way to contact you" (official) ([App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Google requires a policy link in Play Console and in the app, on an "active, publicly accessible and non-geofenced URL (no PDFs)" that is "non-editable". It must name a privacy point of contact, cover data access, collection, use and sharing, and state the retention and deletion policy. "Apps that do not access any personal and sensitive user data must still submit a privacy policy" (official) ([Google Play User Data policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10144311)). Google's Families policy adds that the policy must accurately reflect data practices (official) ([Google Play Families policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335)). CalOPPA requires a conspicuously posted policy from any site collecting PII from California residents, a disclosure of how the operator responds to Do Not Track, and a statement on whether third parties collect PII through the site (commentary from 2013-2014; the statute was not retrieved) ([Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/california-adopts-do-not-track-disclosure-law-a-b-370-amends-the-california-online-privacy-protection-act-caloppa-to-require-new-privacy-policy-disclosures-for-websites-online-services-and-mobile/)).

Neither store requires the policy to cover the website, but the site will collect a contact form, a waitlist, analytics, and CDN logs containing IP addresses, so CalOPPA, GDPR transparency and FTC truthfulness all apply to it. The recommended structure (inference) is a single `/privacy` page with these sections: who we are and how to reach us; the Math Dragons app (what it collects, no ads, no accounts, no third-party SDKs, children's privacy statement); the website (form, waitlist, analytics vendor, hosting logs, retention periods); the DNT response ("we don't track visitors across other sites"); third parties that receive site data; deletion requests; and the effective date and change process. Migration from apps.routeworks.app/math-dragons requires four things: updating the URL in App Store Connect and Play Console, a 301 from the old URL, pointing the in-app link at the new URL in the next build, and keeping the old URL redirecting at least until every build containing the old in-app link has expired. A support or contact page with a working form and a plain email address satisfies Apple's Support URL requirement.

One factual conflict must be resolved before the policy is written. The brief for this report says the app collects no data. The companion video report states that Math Dragons uses **Firebase anonymous auth and optional cloud sync**. Both cannot be fully true. Anonymous auth and sync typically create an identifier and store progress server-side (inference), which affects the app section of the policy, the App Store "Data Not Collected" label, the Play Data safety form, and every "collects no data" line on the site.

### Cookieless is not consent-free everywhere, but the exposure is small

The US has no general cookie-banner law; disclosure in the privacy policy satisfies CalOPPA (inference from the sources above). The EU is stricter on paper. The EDPB's Guidelines 2/2023 (v2.0, adopted October 7, 2024) state that distributing JavaScript that instructs a device to send identifiers "constitutes a 'gaining of access'" under ePrivacy Article 5(3), that locally produced information is still covered, and that this "does not systematically mean that consent needs to be collected", leaving exemptions to case-by-case assessment (official) ([EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 v2](https://edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202302_technical_scope_art_53_eprivacydirective_v2_en_0.pdf)). Cookieless JS analytics is therefore not automatically exempt in the EU, and nothing should call it "GDPR-exempt". The UK moved the other way: the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 adds a PECR exemption for storage used solely for statistical analytics to improve a service, provided users get clear information and "a means of objecting", with the main provisions commencing **February 5, 2026** (commentary; one vendor dates full force to June 19, 2026, which appears to be the final tranche) ([Bratby Law](https://bratby.law/data-use-and-access-act-2025-commencement/); [Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/data-use-and-access-act-2025-duaa-compliance/)).

For a small US site with no EU targeting, the proportionate answer (inference) is first-party cookieless analytics, a clear notice in the privacy policy, and an opt-out link in the footer. That satisfies the UK exemption's "means of objecting" and gives EU visitors a meaningful choice without a banner. Skipping the analytics script entirely when the browser sends Global Privacy Control or Do Not Track is cheap, gives the CalOPPA DNT disclosure a concrete answer, and costs little data at beta scale. A geo-targeted EU consent prompt is the stricter fallback if EU traffic becomes material. Vendor "no cookie banner required" claims are claims, not rulings ([Umami pricing](https://umami.is/pricing); [Plausible](https://plausible.io/#pricing)). Sources also conflict on whether Cloudflare's own beacon ever set an identifying cookie: Cloudflare says it uses no cookies or localStorage and no fingerprinting ([Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/free-privacy-first-analytics-for-a-better-web/)), while an undated independent review reported one ([ctrl.blog](https://www.ctrl.blog/entry/review-cloudflare-analytics/)). The only reliable check is to inspect cookies, localStorage and network calls in DevTools on the deployed site before publishing any "no cookies" line. Cloudflare Turnstile is a related trap: it is classified as strictly necessary, but a `cf.turnstile.u` cookie has been flagged loading before consent ([Webflow forum](https://discourse.webflow.com/t/cookie-consent-compliance-cf-turnstile-u-cookie-loading-before-consent/326947)). It should load only on the contact page, and only if a honeypot and rate limiting prove insufficient.

The UK ICO Children's Code applies to services "likely to be accessed" by children [not re-verified this session]. A parent-directed site arguably falls outside it, but kid-styled art cuts the other way. The site's existing posture (no tracking, no profiling, high-privacy defaults) is the mitigation either way.

### The waitlist needs double opt-in and a postal address

US commercial email falls under CAN-SPAM [not re-verified this session; source to fetch: the [FTC CAN-SPAM guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)]. That means accurate headers and subjects, a working opt-out honored within 10 business days, and a **valid physical postal address** in every commercial message. A P.O. box or commercial mail receiving agency keeps Justin's home address out of parent inboxes. In the EU and UK, marketing email to individuals effectively needs freely given, unticked-box consent with a record of it. Double opt-in is the norm and the strongest evidence of consent rather than a universal mandate, and it also stops a child or a third party from signing up someone else's address (commentary and inference, from the compliance notes above). The contact form should collect the minimum (email and message, name optional), state its purpose ("so we can reply"), carry a retention period, and never add a sender to the waitlist without a separate unticked opt-in.

## Claims, perks and badges must survive a literal reading

### "Practice" is safe, "improves" needs a study, and "COPPA compliant" is a legal conclusion

FTC Section 5 requires claims to be truthful and substantiated before they are made. The FTC has acted against children's educational and cognitive-benefit claims: Focus Education's Jungle Rangers game claimed it permanently improved "focus, memory, attention, behavior, and school performance" without reliable evidence (commentary, circa 2015) ([Olshan](https://www.olshanlaw.com/Advertising-Law-Blog/ifocus-FTC-Brain-Training-Claims)). WordSmart's misrepresentation of educational benefits produced an $18.7M judgment, suspended on payment of $147,400 (commentary) ([Cohen Healthcare Law](https://cohenhealthcarelaw.com/?p=7065)). The FTC also acted against **Miniclip in 2020** for claiming membership in CARU's COPPA safe harbor after CARU had terminated it (official) ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2020/05/swiss-digital-game-developer-settles-ftc-allegations-it-falsely)), and its FAQ warns that misrepresenting the oversight provided for a child-directed app "could be a deceptive practice" (official) ([FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions)). Apple applies the same standard off-store: misleading marketing "whether within or outside of the App Store" is grounds for removal under 2.3.1(a) (official) ([App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). The most recent educational-claims case found dates to about 2015; newer actions were not checked.

The resulting claims ladder (inference, consistent with the video report's) has three rungs. Feature descriptions are safe: "practice addition, subtraction, multiplication and division with difficulty that adapts". Moderate claims need documentation: "covers topics from [standard]", only if mapped. Outcome claims are off-limits without a study of the app itself: "improves scores", "proven", "learn faster". Parent testimonials that state outcomes count as the developer's claims under the Endorsement Guides [not re-verified this session]. "Safe" should always be unpacked into specifics. "COPPA compliant" should never be a badge or headline; "built to collect no personal information from children" is factual and checkable. No safe harbor seal should ever appear without a current certification. "No data collected" must be scoped to the thing it is literally true of: "The Math Dragons app collects no data" (once the Firebase question is settled), never "We collect no data", because the website does collect data.

### Tester perks are allowed only as thank-yous, never as review incentives

Apple's 2.2 says TestFlight apps "cannot be distributed to testers in exchange for compensation of any kind, including as a reward for crowd-sourced funding". Section 3 threatens expulsion for "paid, incentivized, filtered, or fake feedback" (official) ([App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Google prohibits "incentivizing feedback" within its ratings-and-reviews manipulation policy, gives "asking users to rate your app while offering an incentive" as a violation, and says nothing about tester incentives (official) ([Google Play policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9898684)). The plain reading of Apple's rule is a bar on testers paying for beta access (for example as a Kickstarter reward), not on the developer thanking testers. Google's "incentivizing feedback" most naturally means public store feedback, but it could be read more broadly (inference). The safe pattern is a perk granted to every beta participant regardless of what they say, described as a thank-you, and never connected to a rating, a review, a survey answer or a purchase.

"Lifetime free access" is the riskiest phrasing. The app is free with no in-app purchases today, so the promise has no store mechanics behind it. If monetization arrives, honoring it on iOS would need Apple's systems (offer codes or a StoreKit unlock), and an honor-system unlock could conflict with 3.1.1 [not re-verified this session]. A promise that cannot be delivered becomes misleading marketing under 2.3.1(a). A specific, deliverable perk ("every beta family gets the Founding Rider dragon skin when it launches") is safer. Testers who post about the app after receiving a perk should disclose it, under the FTC Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule (effective October 2024) and the Endorsement Guides [not re-verified this session].

### No store badges until the app is publicly listed

Apple licenses its badge artwork "only in connection with Your applications that are available for download on the App Store". Its only pre-release variant is the pre-order badge, and its marketing guidelines contain **no TestFlight badge** at all (official) ([Apple Marketing Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/)). "Download on the App Store" is therefore off-limits during a TestFlight-only beta. It also cannot be used to link to TestFlight itself, because TestFlight is Apple's app, not "Your application" (inference). Google's badges "promote content on Google Play" and include a pre-registration variant, but the retrieved page says nothing about closed testing (official) ([Google Partner Marketing Hub](https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google-play/google-play/lockups-icons-badges/#badges)). A closed-test opt-in is not a public listing, so the standard badge implies availability that does not exist (inference). During the beta, the site should use plain text buttons ("Join the iPhone beta (TestFlight)", "Join the Android beta"), with "TestFlight" carrying a trademark symbol on first US mention and appearing in an Apple credit line in the footer. Apple's guidelines require a symbol on first mention in US body copy and credit lines wherever legal notices appear (official) ([Apple Marketing Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/)). TestFlight's exact symbol (TM or R) was not confirmed.

At public launch the official badges replace the buttons, unmodified. Apple's rules: minimum 40 px onscreen, clear space of one-quarter badge height, no animation, one badge per layout. Google's: minimum 28 px, same clear space, no recoloring or rearranging (official) ([Apple Marketing Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/marketing/guidelines/); [Google Partner Marketing Hub](https://partnermarketinghub.withgoogle.com/brands/google-play/google-play/lockups-icons-badges/#badges)). Device imagery is constrained too: Apple bezels only, no iPhone beside a competing device in the same image, and multi-platform references "only in copy or with badges" (official) (same Apple source). Google's current attribution line was not retrieved.

### WCAG 2.2 AA is cheap now and the lawsuit volume is real

No US regulation names a WCAG version for private websites, but plaintiffs and courts use WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA as the benchmark. UsableNet counted **more than 5,000 digital accessibility lawsuits in 2025**, and Seyfarth counted **3,117 federal website suits in 2025, up 27% from 2,452**, with about 70% against e-commerce and retail and 46% against repeat defendants (secondary summaries of those reports) ([Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/news/digital-accessibility-lawsuits-surge-in-2025-22618502); [TestParty](https://testparty.ai/blog/ada-lawsuit-trends-ecommerce-2025-2026-data)). A non-transactional informational site carries lower risk, and no data was found on suits against solo developers. Building to 2.2 AA on a static site is nonetheless a few days of discipline rather than a retrofit (inference).

The checklist that matters here (WCAG criterion numbers from prior knowledge, not re-fetched) covers six areas. Media: alt text on meaningful dragon art and empty alt on decorative art, captions on every video, a transcript for any origin-story video, no autoplay with sound, and a pause control for motion over five seconds. Contrast: 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for UI and focus indicators, which bright kid palettes often fail. Targets and focus: 24x24 px minimum targets (store badges already exceed this) and visible focus not hidden by a sticky header. Forms: visible labels, text error messages, no puzzle CAPTCHA, and no redundant re-entry. Structure and motion: semantic headings and landmarks, descriptive link text, a consistent help location, `prefers-reduced-motion` honored for animated dragons, and reflow at 320 px and 200% zoom. Process: a published accessibility statement with a contact, axe or Lighthouse checks plus one keyboard pass and one VoiceOver or NVDA pass, and no overlay widgets [overlay litigation claim not re-verified this session].

## Astro on Cloudflare Workers runs the whole funnel for $0-9 a month

### Cloudflare static assets are free and unlimited, and the rivals all have a catch

Cloudflare's compatibility matrix (fetched 2026-10-19) shows **Workers with static assets** supporting redirects, custom headers, middleware, preview URLs, rollbacks, gradual deployments, Workers Logs, rate limiting and Email Workers bindings, several of which Pages lacks. Pages keeps two advantages: custom domains outside Cloudflare zones and full Early Hints (official) ([Cloudflare compatibility matrix](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/static-assets/compatibility-matrix/)). `_redirects` and `_headers` work natively in the asset directory (official) ([Cloudflare migration guide](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/static-assets/migration-guides/migrate-from-pages/)). Older posts claiming Workers cannot do redirects are outdated. Pricing is the clincher: "Requests to static assets are free and unlimited", Workers Free allows 100,000 invocations a day, and Paid starts at $5 a month (official) ([Cloudflare Workers pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/)). Cloudflare's own pages frame Workers as the migration target from Pages without literally saying "use Workers for new projects"; only third-party 2026 guides say that outright (secondary) ([Mecanik.dev](https://mecanik.dev/en/posts/cloudflare-pages-vs-workers-which-to-use-in-2026/)).

The alternatives each fail on a specific term. Vercel Hobby is "restricted to non-commercial personal use only", and commercial use includes "advertising the sale of a product or service" (official, page updated 2026-09-14) ([Vercel Fair Use Guidelines](https://vercel.com/docs/limits/fair-use-guidelines)). A marketing site for a future-monetized app plausibly falls inside that definition, so Vercel means Pro at $20 a month per seat ([Vercel Pricing](https://vercel.com/pricing)). Netlify Free is now 300 credits, with bandwidth at 20 credits per GB and each production deploy at 15 credits (official) ([Netlify Pricing](https://www.netlify.com/pricing/)). That caps the free tier at roughly 15 GB a month with zero deploys, which a breakout Short could exhaust (inference). GitHub Pages is "not intended for or allowed to be used as a free web-hosting service to run your online business" (official) ([GitHub Pages limits](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/github-pages-limits)).

### Astro is now Cloudflare's own framework and ships zero JavaScript by default

Cloudflare acquired The Astro Technology Company on **January 16, 2026**. Astro remains MIT-licensed and host-agnostic ([Business Wire](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260116386991/en/Cloudflare-Acquires-Astro-to-Accelerate-the-Future-of-High-Performance-Web-Development)). Astro 6 stable shipped around **March 10, 2026**; one source says February 10, which appears to be the beta date ([Bytes #469](https://bytes.dev/archives/469); [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/astro-v6-beta-cloudflare)). Astro 6 stabilized Live Content Collections, the Fonts API and a CSP API, and rebuilt the `@astrojs/cloudflare` adapter to run workerd in dev, prerender and production ([Astro 6 Beta blog](https://astro.build/blog/astro-6-beta/)). For this site, build-time content collections over MDX files beat live collections, since nothing needs to be real-time. The stable CSP API gives a hash-based Content Security Policy without hand-maintained script hashes, which suits a privacy-first site (inference). The comparison with alternatives is background knowledge, not re-verified: Next.js static export ships a React runtime that is wasted bytes in a slow in-app browser, Eleventy is equally fast but lacks typed collections and an integrated image pipeline, and SvelteKit's app-like strengths are not needed for six pages.

### Analytics must support custom events, which rules out Cloudflare's free tool

Cloudflare Web Analytics cannot do custom events ("Not yet") and does not log query strings, so it can see neither a "join iPhone" tap nor a `?src=yt1` value (official) ([Cloudflare Web Analytics FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/web-analytics/faq/)). It works only as a free pageview sanity check. **Umami Cloud's free Hobby tier** gives 100K events a month, one site, six-month retention, custom events and no cookies (vendor, fetched 2026-10-19). A third-party guide's claim of 1M free events conflicts with Umami's own page, which wins ([Umami pricing](https://umami.is/pricing)). **Plausible Starter costs $9 a month**, with custom events, native UTM campaign reporting with channel grouping, and an AGPL self-hostable edition (vendor) ([Plausible](https://plausible.io/#pricing)); funnels are reportedly Business-tier only (secondary) ([classcub](https://tt.classcub.com/blog/plausible-vs-fathom-vs-matomo)). PostHog can run cookieless with in-memory persistence, at the cost of a new anonymous ID per page load (official) ([PostHog persistence docs](https://posthog.com/docs/libraries/js/persistence)). That reduces it to Plausible-level counting with a heavier script. Fathom (~$15/mo) and Simple Analytics (~$19-20/mo) were priced only from secondary sources.

### Attribution happens on the site, because the stores pass nothing back

Neither TestFlight public links nor Play opt-in links return install attribution to the site (inference; Play opt-in referrer pass-through was not verified). The conversion proxy is therefore the outbound tap, counted by platform and by source, backed up by TestFlight's install and session counts, Play opt-in counts, and the welcome-survey "where did you hear about us?" answer. Two designs work. In the cheap one, a `_redirects` rule such as `/yt1 /?utm_source=youtube&utm_medium=shorts&utm_campaign=yt1 302` costs zero Worker invocations, Plausible and Umami read the UTMs automatically, and 302 rather than 301 keeps paths repointable (inference, built on the official `_redirects` support above). The better one prerenders each campaign path as a real page from a campaigns data file. That gives per-video message match, removes a redirect hop on slow in-app connections, and lets analytics see `/yt1` directly, with a canonical tag to `/` and `noindex` to avoid duplicate-content issues (inference). In both cases the source value stays in the URL and in page memory, is attached as a property to the `join_click` event (`{platform, src}`), and is appended to internal links. Under the EDPB reading, avoiding sessionStorage and cookies sidesteps the storage question entirely (inference).

Ad blockers will hide some script-based events, so a server-side counter is worth its tiny cost (inference). Outbound buttons point at `/go/ios?src=yt1` and `/go/android?src=yt1`, a few-line Worker writes the hit to Workers Analytics Engine without storing an IP or identifier, and then 302s to TestFlight or the Google Group. Each tap is one free-tier invocation. The same pattern also gives one place to repoint links when a TestFlight build or Play URL changes, which matters because the videos are evergreen. Path naming needs one convention across both reports: this brief uses `/yt1`, while the video report suggested `/yt-012`. Zero-padded, platform-prefixed paths (`/yt012`, `/fb012`) stay typeable and sort cleanly (inference).

### Device detection picks the highlighted button and never hides the other

Detection runs client-side in a few lines of inline JavaScript, so pages stay static and cacheable; server-side UA routing would cost a Worker invocation per hit and break caching (inference). The `inapp-spy` library (v5.0.10) detects Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, Threads, TikTok, the Google app and others but not YouTube, and its maintainer advises to "always test on real devices" ([inapp-spy README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/inapp-spy@5.0.10/README.md)). A hand-rolled check for `FBAN|FBAV|Instagram` is enough here. Three facts are background knowledge, not sourced. YouTube's absence from the token list is because it opens SFSafariViewController on iOS and Chrome Custom Tabs on Android, which are full browsers. iPadOS Safari reports a Macintosh UA by default, so iPads need a `navigator.maxTouchPoints > 1` check. UA Client Hints are Chromium-only and absent on iOS, so they cannot be the primary signal. With no JavaScript or an unknown device, both paths render equally.

### Forms, email, OG and QA stay on one vendor where possible

The contact and feedback forms post to a Worker at `/api/contact` that validates a honeypot field and time-to-submit, applies the Workers Rate Limiting binding, and forwards to Justin's inbox via an Email Workers binding or an email API. Turnstile is added only if spam appears. Turnstile states it processes only data strictly necessary for bot detection and does not access form entries, but its Pre-Clearance mode issues a cookie (official) ([Turnstile FAQ](https://developers.cloudflare.com/turnstile/frequently-asked-questions/)). Web3Forms (250 free submissions a month, bundled hCaptcha) is the zero-backend alternative, at the cost of another third party (secondary) ([splitforms](https://splitforms.com/blog/web3forms-vs-formspree-vs-splitforms)). For the waitlist, secondary 2026 sources list **EmailOctopus free to 2,500 subscribers**, **Kit's free plan to 10,000**, **Buttondown free to 100 then from $9/mo**, and **Loops free to 1,000** ([devtoolpicks](https://devtoolpicks.com/blog/best-mailchimp-alternatives-indie-hackers-2026); [EmailOctopus blog](https://emailoctopus.com/blog/best-free-email-marketing-tools)). None of these vendor pages were fetched. The deciding features are built-in double opt-in, a postal-address footer, email-only signup, and the ability to export. Storing emails in D1 until launch avoids an ESP but means hand-building confirmation and unsubscribe flows (inference).

No sources were fetched for OG images, structured data or QA tooling, so the following is background knowledge to verify against current docs. OG images are 1200x630 PNGs prerendered at build time with satori plus resvg (which needs a TTF or OTF font, not WOFF2) or an Astro OG package, and use absolute `og:image` URLs, since Meta's scraper caches aggressively and needs the Sharing Debugger to refresh. Add `@astrojs/sitemap`, canonical tags, `MobileApplication` JSON-LD with `offers` at price 0, and `Article` JSON-LD on future articles; a software-app rich result is unlikely without ratings. QR codes are generated at build time as inline SVG with the `qrcode` package. For QA: Lighthouse CI on mobile throttling in GitHub Actions with budgets such as LCP under 2.0 s, CLS under 0.05 and total JS under 20 KB. Playwright covers device descriptors plus spoofed `FBAN`/`Instagram` UAs for highlight logic, asserts each short path's 302 and UTM `Location`, and runs `@axe-core/playwright` on every page. A manual matrix then has Justin open an unlisted Short and a private Reel or DM on a real iPhone and a real Android phone and complete both join flows end to end, because UA spoofing cannot reproduce webview store hand-offs.

## Conclusion

The site's real product is the hand-off, not the page. Everything that makes a marketing page persuasive (a matching hero, short copy, honest trust signals) is worth less than whether a parent in Facebook's webview, signed into the wrong Google account, makes it to an installed beta. That shifts effort toward the unglamorous pieces: the in-app escape banner, the account-mismatch line, the server-side click counter, a status banner for lapsed builds, and a real-device test pass on every release. It also means the site's numbers should be read per step. A low end-to-end rate is expected; a low tap rate on step 2 for Android-in-Facebook is a fixable bug.

The second insight is that compliance and brand discipline converge again, as they did for the video channel. Addressing adults, keeping Tallyclaw as a narrator rather than a playmate, avoiding child activities, and stating privacy as scoped, checkable facts protect the site's COPPA posture, keep the videos out of Made for Kids, and satisfy the FTC and Apple's off-store marketing rule in one move. The one place that discipline is currently at risk is the core promise itself: until the Firebase anonymous-auth and cloud-sync question is answered, "the app collects no data" is the claim most likely to be contradicted by the app's own store labels, and it should not ship on the site until it is verified.
