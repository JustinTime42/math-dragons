# Narrator voice clone: reading script

## How to record (read this, don't record it)

- **Room:** record in a quiet room with soft furnishings (a bedroom or a closet full of clothes works well). Turn off fans, heaters, fridges you can hear, and phone notifications. No background music or TV.
- **Same setup every time:** same mic, same room, same chair, same distance from the mic (about a hand's width, a little off to the side to soften the "p" sounds). Note the setup so you can repeat it exactly.
- **Delivery:** natural and calm, as if you're explaining something to a friend at the kitchen table. Don't perform and don't put on a "narrator voice". The clone copies whatever you do, so do what you want it to do.
- **Volume:** keep it steady. Don't trail off at the ends of sentences, and don't get louder when you get excited. Let the words carry the emotion.
- **Pauses:** pause naturally between paragraphs, about a breath. Between sections, stop the take or leave a couple of seconds of quiet.
- **Mistakes:** if you flub a sentence, pause, then re-read the whole sentence. It's fine. Trim the flub later, or leave it if your editor makes that hard; a few clean re-reads won't hurt.
- **Sessions:** split it across as many sessions as you like (one section at a time is fine), but keep the setup identical. Same time of day helps the voice sound the same. Have water nearby, skip dairy before recording.
- **Export and limits:** export high-quality audio (WAV, or MP3 at 192 kbps or higher), no compression, no effects, no noise filters. Follow ElevenLabs' own current minimums and guidance shown in the app; they change, and the app is the source of truth.

Read only the plain paragraphs. Skip headings and anything in *[square brackets]*.

## Section B: Adding, making ten, and number bonds (about 6 minutes)

Here's a small thing that makes a big difference. Before adding gets quick, it helps to know the pairs that make ten. One and nine. Two and eight. Three and seven. Four and six. Five and five. That's the whole list, if you don't count flipping them around. Five pairs.

Some worksheets call these number bonds. It's a fancy name for a simple idea. A number can be split into parts, and the parts put back together make the whole. Ten can be seven and three. Ten can be six and four. Ten can also be ten and zero, which feels like cheating, but it counts.

Why does ten get so much attention? Because our numbers are built on it. Ten ones make a ten. Ten tens make a hundred. Ten hundreds make a thousand. Once your kid is comfortable getting to ten, a lot of other adding gets easier.

Take nine plus five. Nine is one away from ten. So borrow one from the five. Nine plus one is ten, and four is left. Ten plus four is fourteen. Same answer you'd get counting on your fingers, just with fewer steps.

Try seven plus six. Seven needs three more to make ten. Split the six into three and three. Seven plus three is ten. Ten plus three is thirteen.

Or eight plus seven. Eight needs two. Split the seven into two and five. Eight plus two is ten. Ten plus five is fifteen.

If that sounds slow when you say it out loud, that's normal. It's slow the first few times. Then it gets quicker. Then one day it disappears into your kid's head, and they just know that eight plus seven is fifteen.

There's another trick that sits right next to this one. Doubles. Doubles are easy to picture, so they tend to stick early. Two plus two is four. Three plus three is six. Four plus four is eight. Five plus five is ten, which is both hands. Six plus six is twelve, which is a carton of eggs. Seven plus seven is fourteen. Eight plus eight is sixteen. Nine plus nine is eighteen. Ten plus ten is twenty.

Once the doubles are solid, you get the near doubles almost for free. Six plus seven? That's six plus six, plus one more. Twelve, then thirteen. Seven plus eight? Seven plus seven is fourteen, plus one is fifteen. Eight plus nine? Eight plus eight is sixteen, plus one, seventeen.

So now there are two roads to seven plus eight. Make a ten, or use a double. Both get you to fifteen. Neither road is the right one. If your kid picks one and it works, that's a good night.

Subtraction works the same way, in reverse. Take fourteen minus six. You could count back six, but that's a lot of counting, and it's easy to lose your place. Or you can go down to ten first. Fourteen minus four is ten. You still owe two more. Ten minus two is eight. So fourteen minus six is eight.

Here's another way that surprises people. Count up instead of taking away. For fifty-two minus forty-eight, don't take anything away at all. Start at forty-eight and count up to fifty-two. Forty-nine, fifty, fifty-one, fifty-two. That's four steps. So fifty-two minus forty-eight is four. When two numbers are close together, counting up is often the quickest way there.

Every subtraction fact has an addition fact hiding inside it. If your kid knows six plus eight is fourteen, then they also know fourteen minus six is eight, and fourteen minus eight is six. One fact, three answers. It's a good deal.

A quick word about bigger numbers. When your kid sees forty-seven, it helps to hear it as four tens and seven ones. Add twenty to that, and only the tens change. Four tens become six tens. Sixty-seven. Add three to forty-seven, and the ones fill up to ten. Fifty. That's all place value really means, and it's the reason the ones column and the tens column exist.

Here's a fun one to try at dinner. What's ninety-nine plus forty-seven? It looks hard. But ninety-nine is one short of a hundred. Take one from the forty-seven and give it to the ninety-nine. Now it's a hundred plus forty-six. One hundred forty-six. Done.

The same idea works with money. Something costs four dollars and ninety-nine cents, and another thing costs three dollars. Call the first one five dollars, add three, that's eight, then take the penny back. Seven dollars and ninety-nine cents.

And if you want to know whether an answer is in the right neighborhood, round first. Thirty-eight plus forty-one is close to forty plus forty, so the answer should be near eighty. It's seventy-nine. Close enough to trust. If your kid gets an answer of seven hundred and nine, you both know something slipped.

One last adding trick, because it's oddly satisfying. Adding nine is the same as adding ten and taking one away. Twenty-six plus nine? Twenty-six plus ten is thirty-six. Take away one. Thirty-five. Forty-three plus nine? Fifty-three, then fifty-two. Once you see it, you can't unsee it.

## Section C: Even, odd, and the strange case of zero (about 4 minutes)

Even and odd come up early, and they keep coming up.

The rule I like best is the simple one. A number is even if it splits into two equal groups with none left over. Eight splits into four and four. Even. Nine splits into four and five. Or think of it as four and four, with one left over. Odd.

There's a shortcut, too. Look at the last digit. If it's zero, two, four, six or eight, the whole number is even. If it's one, three, five, seven or nine, it's odd. So three hundred fifty-six is even, and you don't have to split anything to know it. Seven thousand and one is odd. A number with fifty digits is even or odd depending only on the very last one. The other forty-nine don't get a vote.

Here's a pattern kids tend to enjoy noticing. Even plus even is always even. Four plus six is ten. Odd plus odd is also even, which surprises people. Three plus five is eight. Seven plus nine is sixteen. And even plus odd is always odd. Four plus seven is eleven. Twelve plus five is seventeen.

Why does odd plus odd come out even? Picture each odd number as pairs, plus one extra. Put two odd numbers together, and the two extras make a new pair. Nothing's left over anymore. I think that's a lovely little argument, and it's real math. It's a proof, and it fits in a sentence.

Multiplying has its own pattern. An even number times anything is even. Six times seven is forty-two. Even. Two times nine is eighteen. Even. The only way to get an odd answer is odd times odd. Three times five is fifteen. Seven times nine is sixty-three. Odd.

And then there's zero. Zero is even. I know. It's a very common thing to get wrong, and grown-ups get it wrong too, so nobody needs to feel bad about it. But split zero into two equal groups and you get zero and zero, with nothing left over. Count down by twos from ten: ten, eight, six, four, two, zero. Zero lands right where the evens go. And the numbers on either side of it, one and negative one, are both odd. Even numbers sit between odd ones. Zero fits the pattern perfectly.

While we're here, a word about two. Two is the only even prime number. A prime number has exactly two factors: one, and itself. Seven is prime. Its only factors are one and seven. Every even number bigger than two can be divided by two, so it has at least three factors, and that rules it out. Two gets to be the odd one out among the primes, even though it's even. Tallyclaw would want me to say that with a straight face.

And one is not prime. It only has one factor, which is one. Mathematicians today agree on this, partly because it keeps a lot of other rules tidy.

The first few primes are two, three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen, seventeen, nineteen, twenty-three and twenty-nine. Nine looks like it should be on the list, but three times three is nine, so it's out. Fifteen is three times five. Twenty-one is three times seven. Fifty-one is a sneaky one. It looks prime, but it's three times seventeen.

## Section D: Times tables and division (about 6 minutes)

Seven times eight is fifty-six. It has a reputation, and kids and grown-ups both get stuck on it.

Here's a way to remember it. Say five, six, seven, eight. Fifty-six is seven times eight. Five, six, seven, eight. The numbers line up in order. It's a silly trick, and it works.

The times table looks big. One times one up to ten times ten is a hundred boxes. But a lot of it repeats. Three times seven is the same as seven times three. Count each pair once, and you're down to fifty-five. Take out the ones and the tens, which are easy, and there are thirty-six left. That's a lot less scary than a hundred.

Then you can knock out more with a few patterns.

The twos are doubles. Two times eight is eight plus eight. Sixteen. If your kid knows their doubles, they already know the twos.

The fours are doubles of doubles. Four times six? Double six is twelve. Double twelve is twenty-four. Four times seven? Double seven is fourteen. Double fourteen is twenty-eight.

The eights are one more double after that. Eight times six: six, twelve, twenty-four, forty-eight. Three doubles. Eight times six is forty-eight.

The fives always end in zero or five, and they're half of the tens. Five times eight is half of ten times eight. Half of eighty is forty. Five times seven is half of seventy. Thirty-five. Five times nine is half of ninety. Forty-five.

The tens put a zero on the end. Ten times six is sixty. Ten times nine is ninety. Ten times ten is one hundred.

The nines have the finger trick, and they have another pattern too. From nine times one up to nine times ten, the digits of every answer add up to nine. Eighteen: one plus eight is nine. Twenty-seven: two plus seven is nine. Thirty-six, forty-five, fifty-four, sixty-three, seventy-two, eighty-one, ninety. And the tens digit is always one less than the number you're multiplying by. Nine times six? The tens digit is five. Five and four make nine. Fifty-four.

The threes and sixes are related. Six times anything is double three times that thing. Three times seven is twenty-one, so six times seven is forty-two. Three times eight is twenty-four, so six times eight is forty-eight.

Squares are worth a moment. One, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-five, thirty-six, forty-nine, sixty-four, eighty-one, one hundred. Six times six is thirty-six. Seven times seven is forty-nine. Eight times eight is sixty-four. They run down the diagonal of the times table, and they make good landmarks.

Landmarks help with the hard ones. If your kid knows six times six is thirty-six, then six times seven is just one more six. Forty-two. If they know seven times seven is forty-nine, then seven times eight is one more seven. Fifty-six. There it is again.

When you take away all the patterns, the ones left over are usually a handful in the sixes, sevens and eights. Six times seven. Six times eight. Seven times eight. Those are worth a little extra practice, and that's fine. A few hard facts is a normal number of hard facts.

Now division. Division is multiplication running backward. Twelve divided by three asks a question: how many threes make twelve? Three, six, nine, twelve. Four threes. So twelve divided by three is four.

You can also think of it as sharing. Twelve cookies, three people. Each person gets four. Same answer, different picture. Both pictures are worth having, because some problems read like groups and some read like sharing.

Every multiplication fact hands you two division facts. Six times seven is forty-two, so forty-two divided by six is seven, and forty-two divided by seven is six. Learn one, get three. Fifty-six divided by eight is seven. Sixty-three divided by nine is seven. Eighty-one divided by nine is nine.

Sometimes it doesn't come out even. Fourteen divided by four. Four, eight, twelve. That's three fours, with two left over. So fourteen divided by four is three, remainder two. The leftover has to be smaller than the number you're dividing by. If it isn't, you could have made another group.

Order matters in division, even though it doesn't in multiplication. Three times four and four times three are both twelve. But twelve divided by three is four, while three divided by twelve is one quarter. Same numbers, very different answers.

Then there's zero again. Anything times zero is zero. Seven times zero is zero. A thousand times zero is zero. Zero divided by five is also zero. Five friends, no cookies, nobody gets anything. But five divided by zero doesn't work at all. You can't share five cookies among zero people and say how many each person got. No number fits, so we say dividing by zero is undefined. It's a rare spot where math just says no.

And one last pattern. Anything times one stays the same. Anything divided by one stays the same. Anything divided by itself is one, as long as it isn't zero. Eight divided by eight is one. A hundred divided by a hundred is one.

## Section E: Fractions are sharing (about 3 minutes)

Fractions have a reputation. But kids meet fractions long before school gives them a name. Anyone who's split a sandwich with a sibling knows exactly what half means, and has strong opinions about whether it was fair.

The simplest way in is this: a fraction is a fair share. One pizza, two people. Each gets one half. One pizza, four people. Each gets one quarter, also called one fourth. One pizza, eight people. Each gets one eighth.

Now notice what happens. More people, smaller slices. One eighth is smaller than one quarter, even though eight is bigger than four. This is the part that trips a lot of kids up. The bottom number tells you how many equal pieces the whole is cut into. More pieces means each piece is smaller.

The top number tells you how many of those pieces you have. Three quarters means the pizza was cut into four equal pieces, and you have three of them. Two thirds means it was cut into three, and you have two. Five eighths means eight pieces, and you've got five.

Fractions that look different can be the same amount. Two quarters is one half. Cut the pizza into four, take two pieces, and you've got half the pizza. Four eighths is one half too. So is five tenths. So is fifty hundredths. They all land in the same place.

Halves and quarters of numbers make a good bridge. Half of eight is four. Half of ten is five. Half of fifty is twenty-five. A quarter of twelve is three, because twelve shared four ways is three each. A third of twelve is four. A third of fifteen is five.

Money helps here. Fifty cents is half a dollar. Twenty-five cents is a quarter of a dollar, which is where the coin gets its name. Four quarters make a dollar. Three quarters is seventy-five cents.

Here's a quick one for dinner. Which is more, one third of a pizza or one quarter of the same pizza? One third. Fewer people sharing means bigger slices. If your kid says one quarter because four is bigger, that's a really useful mistake. It shows you exactly where the idea is still settling.

When your kid says a fraction, it helps to ask: a half of what? Half of a small pizza and half of a large pizza are not the same amount of pizza. The fraction tells you the share. The whole still matters.

And one more connection, for later. A fraction is also a division. Three quarters is three divided by four. One half is one divided by two. Twelve divided by three is twelve thirds, which is four. It's all one family.

## Section F: For parents: practice without the fight (about 6 minutes)

If math homework has turned into a fight at your house, you're in good company. It happens in a lot of homes. It doesn't mean your kid can't do math, and it doesn't mean you're doing it wrong. Usually it just means everyone is tired.

Here's where I'd start. Short and calm beats long and tense. Five minutes of practice, then stop. Stop while it's still going fine, not after it falls apart. Ending on a good note makes the next day easier to start.

If you only have five minutes, that's plenty. Pick one thing. Maybe it's the pairs that make ten. Maybe it's the fours. Do a few, notice what went well, and call it a night.

Some nights it won't go well, and that's allowed too. If your kid is melting down over six times eight, the fact isn't going anywhere. You can close the book and try tomorrow. Nobody's keeping score.

It's also okay to not know something. If your kid asks how to do a problem and you don't remember, say so. I don't know, let's figure it out. That's a great sentence to say out loud. It shows that not knowing is just the first step. Then look it up together, or try a few numbers and see what happens.

If the worksheet uses a method you never learned, that's normal too. Math is taught a little differently now than when a lot of us were in school. The answers haven't changed. Eight plus six is still fourteen. The worksheet is usually just showing the thinking behind it. If you're stuck on what a method means, it's fine to send the teacher a short note. Most teachers would much rather hear from you than have homework turn into a battle.

When you praise, try praising the how. Instead of saying, you're so smart, try saying what your kid actually did. You split the six into two and four. That was clever. Or, you didn't know that one, so you used a double. Nice. That kind of praise points at something your kid can do again tomorrow. It also makes mistakes less scary, because the focus is on the trying.

If your kid gets something wrong, ask how they got it before you say anything else. Often the thinking is mostly right, and one step slipped. Finding that step together is worth more than just saying the right answer. And sometimes you'll find out they had a perfectly good idea you hadn't thought of.

Talking about math at dinner is one of my favorite tricks, because it doesn't feel like homework. Nobody needs a pencil. Try a question like, if there are four of us and twelve meatballs, how many does everyone get? Or, how many legs are under this table right now? Or, I'm thinking of a number. If I double it, I get fourteen. What's my number?

You can use the stuff around you. Count the stairs on the way up. Guess how many grapes are in the bowl, then count them. Split a pizza into eighths and ask how many slices are left after everyone takes one. If it's a car trip, add up the numbers on a license plate. None of it has to be serious. A little playful goes a long way.

If you feel anxious about math yourself, you're not alone, and you can still help. You don't have to be good at math to be curious about it in front of your kid. Keep homework help short and calm. Then add math that isn't homework. Those dinner questions count.

Try not to say things like, I was never good at math either, as if it runs in the family. I get why people say it. It's meant kindly. But it can sound like a door closing. Everyone can get better at math facts with practice. That includes your kid, and it includes you.

Fingers are fine. Counting on fingers is a tool, and it tends to fade on its own as the facts stick. Same with drawing little dots or tally marks on scrap paper. Let your kid use what works, then ask how they did it.

Be careful with speed. A clock can be fun in a game, when it's part of the play. But a parent standing over a kid with a stopwatch is a different feeling. Let accuracy come first. Speed tends to show up later, once the facts feel familiar.

And keep rewards about the work itself. Notice the effort. Notice the strategy. Celebrate the night they finally got seven times eight. You don't need to pay a dollar a page. The good feeling of getting it is a pretty strong reward on its own.

If you're ever worried that something more is going on, if math seems unusually hard for your kid in a way that doesn't change with practice, talk to your child's teacher or doctor. You know your kid best, and asking is always okay.

Mostly, though, I'd say this. Your kid doesn't need a perfect math tutor at home. They need someone who's on their side, who stays calm, and who thinks five minutes is worth doing. You can be that person tonight.

## Section G: Math history, told plainly (about 6 minutes)

People have been counting for a very long time. One of the oldest objects that might be a counting tool is the Ishango bone, found in what's now the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It's about twenty thousand years old, by the usual estimate, and it has rows of notches cut into it. People still argue about what the marks were for. Some think they're tally marks. Some think they're something more. Nobody knows for sure, and I like that we can just say so.

Our word digit comes from the Latin word for finger. That's not an accident. Fingers were the first calculator, and they're still a good one. Our word calculate comes from the Latin word for a small stone, a pebble, because pebbles were used for counting too.

The ancient Egyptians wrote down math problems on papyrus. One famous example is the Rhind papyrus, which is about thirty-five hundred years old. A scribe named Ahmes copied it from an older text. It has problems about sharing bread and measuring land. The Egyptians wrote most of their fractions as one over something. One half, one third, one quarter. If they needed three quarters, they'd write it as one half plus one quarter.

The Babylonians counted in sixties. That's where our sixty seconds in a minute and sixty minutes in an hour come from, by way of later Greek astronomers.

The Romans used letters for numbers. One is I. Five is V. Ten is X. Fifty is L. A hundred is C. Five hundred is D. A thousand is M. Roman numerals had no symbol for zero. Try multiplying forty-seven by thirty-six in Roman numerals, and you'll see why people eventually switched.

The digits most of the world uses now, zero through nine, grew out of a system developed in India. In the year six hundred twenty-eight, the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta wrote down rules for doing arithmetic with zero as a number. Zero plus a number is that number. A number minus itself is zero. That sounds obvious to us now, but someone had to write it down first.

The system traveled west through the Arabic-speaking world. In the eight hundreds, a scholar in Baghdad named al-Khwarizmi wrote a book about calculating with these Indian numerals. When his work was translated into Latin, his name came along with it. That's where our word algorithm comes from, which means a step-by-step method. Every time your kid does long division, they're running an algorithm named after a man in Baghdad who lived more than a thousand years ago.

In twelve oh two, an Italian mathematician we now call Fibonacci published a book that helped bring these numerals to merchants in Europe. He showed how much easier they made adding, multiplying and keeping accounts. The same book has a puzzle about rabbits that leads to a famous list of numbers. One, one, two, three, five, eight, thirteen, twenty-one, thirty-four. Each number is the sum of the two before it. Fibonacci gets his name on it, but the pattern was known in India centuries earlier.

The signs we use came surprisingly late. The plus and minus signs showed up in print in Germany in fourteen eighty-nine. The equals sign came in fifteen fifty-seven, from a Welsh mathematician named Robert Recorde. He was tired of writing is equal to over and over, so he drew two parallel lines, because, as he put it, no two things can be more equal. I think about that every time I write an equals sign.

The times sign, the little x, was used by an English mathematician named William Oughtred in sixteen thirty-one. The division sign, a line with a dot above and below, was used by a Swiss mathematician named Johann Rahn in sixteen fifty-nine. So for most of history, people did math without any of the symbols on your kid's worksheet.

There's a famous story about the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss as a boy. The usual version says his teacher asked the class to add every number from one to a hundred, and young Gauss had the answer almost right away. The details of that story have grown over the years, so I wouldn't bet on them. But the trick is real, and it's great. Pair the first number with the last. One plus a hundred is one hundred one. Two plus ninety-nine is one hundred one. Three plus ninety-eight, same again. There are fifty pairs, each worth one hundred one. Fifty times one hundred one is five thousand fifty. That's the sum of every number from one to a hundred.

And then there's pi, the number you get when you divide the distance around any circle by the distance across it. It's a little more than three. About three point one four. More than two thousand years ago, the Greek mathematician Archimedes showed that pi is a bit less than three and one seventh, and a bit more than three and ten seventy-firsts. He did it without a calculator, by drawing shapes with more and more sides around a circle and inside it. People have been working out more digits ever since.
